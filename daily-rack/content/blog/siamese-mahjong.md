# Siamese Mahjong: How to Play 2-Player American Mahjong

Two of you, one set of tiles, and nobody else free tonight? You can still play American mahjong. Siamese Mah Jongg® is a two-player version where each of you runs two racks at once and needs two winning hands to take the game. It keeps the whole card in play, so it feels like a real game, not a stripped-down one.

Here's how to set it up, play a turn and keep score, plus a simpler option if you'd rather play one hand each.

> **The short version**
>
> - Two players sit across from each other, with **two racks each**.
> - Each player is dealt **27 tiles**. East draws a 28th and discards first.
> - There's **no Charleston**.
> - Hidden tiles move freely between your two racks. Exposures stay with the hand they belong to.
> - Your **first Mah Jongg** scores and play goes on. Your **second Mah Jongg** ends the game and pays double.

## What Siamese mahjong is

Siamese Mah Jongg is a specific two-player game created by Gladys Grad, who holds a registered trademark on the name ([Missy Mahjong's rules and FAQ, reviewed by Gladys Grad](https://www.missymahjong.com/post/siamese-mah-jongg-official-rules-and-faq)). The official rulebook and an annual Siamese card are sold on [mahjongg.org](https://mahjongg.org/siamese-products/). You don't need the special card to start, though: the game works with any American mahjong card, including the National Mah Jongg League card you already own.

People sometimes say "Siamese" for any two-player mahjong. Strictly, it's this game: two racks, two hands and its own scoring. If your group plays a different version, call it two-player mahjong and agree on the rules before the first tile.

Why two hands? With only two people at the table, a single 13-tile hand leaves most of the set sitting in the wall. Building two hands each puts far more tiles to work, so you're reading the card, choosing between hands and calling discards much as you would in a four-player game.

## What you need

- **One American mahjong set** with 152 tiles, including 8 jokers and 8 flowers. If your set has extra jokers or blanks, set them aside.
- **Four racks**, two per player. (One double rack per player works too.)
- **A card** for each player. Your current NMJL card is fine.
- **One die**, and something to keep score on.

## How to set up and deal

1. **Build the walls.** Each player builds two walls, 19 stacks long and two tiles high. That's 38 tiles per wall, and all 152 tiles across the four walls.
2. **Break the wall.** East rolls one die, counts that many stacks from the right end of East's first wall, and moves them to the tail end. Tiles come from East's walls first, then from the opponent's.
3. **Deal.** Take four tiles at a time until each player has 27: six rounds of four, then three.
4. **Skip the Charleston.** There isn't one in Siamese. With two hands each, you already control plenty of tiles.
5. **East starts.** East draws a 28th tile, arranges both racks and discards.

**Check your count.** Between turns you hold 27 tiles. On your turn, after drawing, you hold 28. A wrong count is costly: guides to the published rules say it makes your hand dead ([The MahJong Circle](https://www.themahjongcircle.com/siamese-mahjong)), or both hands ([The Mahjong Section](https://themahjongsection.com/blogs/the-mahjong-blog/siamese-mahjong-rules)). Spotted right after the deal, it's a misdeal and you simply redeal. So count after the deal, and again whenever something feels off.

Older guides deal 28 tiles to East and 27 to the other player, sometimes with no walls at all. Current rules deal 27 each and have East draw the 28th from the wall.

Short on time? For a casual game, some groups skip the walls and draw from a face-down pool in the middle of the table. That's fine at the kitchen table; tournaments require walls ([The MahJong Circle](https://www.themahjongcircle.com/siamese-mahjong)).

## How a turn works

On your turn you either **draw from the wall** or **claim your opponent's discard**. Then you **discard** one tile and name it, just as in a four-player game.

### Arranging your two racks

Your hidden tiles are one pool. Move them between racks as often as you like while you plan. The real skill is choosing **two hands that don't fight over the same tiles**. If both hands need a pung of 3 Dots, one of them won't get it.

For example (a hypothetical pairing; check your own card), one hand from a consecutive-run line and one from a winds-and-dragons line share very few tiles, so almost every draw has somewhere useful to go.

### Calling discards and exposing

You can call your opponent's discard to expose a pung, kong or quint, following the usual American rules: jokers can stand in for groups of three or more, never for singles or pairs.

Two habits matter more in Siamese than anywhere else:

- **Take the tile first, then expose.** Put the called tile on your rack before you lay the group down. Exposing first can cost you the turn and that hand.
- **Expose on the right rack.** Each exposure belongs to one hand and stays with it. If you put it on the wrong rack, you can move it before you discard. Exposures that end up together but don't make a hand on the card make that hand dead.

### Jokers

You can swap a matching tile for a joker in **any** exposure, yours or your opponent's. There are two exceptions. Jokers in a declared Mah Jongg are locked, and you can't take one from the exposure that made a hand dead.

## Winning: two Mah Jonggs

When one hand is complete, you can **declare it**: expose all 14 tiles, then discard to finish your turn. The game keeps going while you build your second hand, and your opponent keeps playing for theirs.

The game ends when:

- someone **declares their second Mah Jongg**, or
- the **wall runs out** and the last discard is made. If nobody exposed a Mah Jongg at all, it's a wall game.

### Declare now, or wait?

This is the real decision in Siamese. A complete hand only scores once it's exposed, and a hidden winner scores nothing if the wall runs out ([The MahJong Circle](https://www.themahjongcircle.com/siamese-mahjong)). But once you expose it, its tiles and jokers are locked, so you can't borrow from it for your second hand.

A reasonable rule of thumb:

- **Declare soon** when the hand is worth a lot, the wall is getting short, or your opponent looks close to finishing.
- **Hold a little longer** when its jokers are exactly what your second hand needs.

Be careful declaring both hands at once. If you claim two Mah Jonggs and one turns out to be wrong, both hands are dead, you stop playing, and you score −100.

## Scoring Siamese mahjong

Most groups keep a running score through the game and settle the difference at the end. The table below is the published scoring as reported by two 2026 guides that cite the official rules ([The Mahjong Section](https://themahjongsection.com/blogs/the-mahjong-blog/siamese-mahjong-rules) and [The MahJong Circle](https://www.themahjongcircle.com/siamese-mahjong)). If your group plays for stakes, check the official rulebook too.

| What happens | Score |
| --- | --- |
| First Mah Jongg | The hand's value on the card. Jokerless doubles it (except hands made only of singles and pairs). |
| Second Mah Jongg, which ends the game | Double the card value. Jokerless doubles it again, so four times the card value. |
| Both hands declared in the same turn | Both are doubled. |
| You discard the tile that gives your opponent their second Mah Jongg | −25 for you |
| Both of your hands go dead | −100 for you, and the game ends |
| Wall game | +10 each |

### A worked example

Ana and Ben are playing. The card values here are made up to keep the math simple.

1. Ana declares her first Mah Jongg: a 25-point hand with no jokers. Jokerless doubles it: 25 × 2 = **50**.
2. Ben declares his first Mah Jongg: a 30-point hand with jokers, so **30**.
3. Ana completes her second hand, a 25-point hand with one joker, on Ben's discard. A second Mah Jongg doubles: 25 × 2 = **50** for Ana. Ben takes **−25** for throwing the winning tile.

Final scores: Ana has 50 + 50 = **100**. Ben has 30 − 25 = **5**. Ana wins by 95, which is what Ben owes if you settle up at the end.

## Siamese or simpler two-player mahjong?

Siamese isn't the only way to play with two. Some players use a simpler heads-up game: build all four walls, East takes 14 tiles and the other player 13, skip the Charleston, and play for a single hand, like a four-player game with two empty chairs. MahjongCompare describes this as the NMJL's two-player method ([MahjongCompare](https://mahjongcompare.com/learn/2-player-mahjong)); check the League's own rulebook for its exact wording.

| | Siamese Mah Jongg | Simple two-player |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Racks per player | Two | One |
| Starting tiles | 27 each (East draws a 28th) | East 14, the other player 13 |
| Charleston | None | None |
| To win | Two Mah Jonggs | One Mah Jongg |
| Feels like | A full game, with more decisions | A four-player hand with two empty chairs |
| Best for | Couples and regulars who want a real game for two | Beginners practising standard play |

If you're brand new, the simple version keeps everything you learn transferable to a four-player table. Once you know your way around the card, Siamese is the richer game.

## Tips for your first game

- **Agree on the rules first.** Walls or a pool, which card, and the scoring above. "Two-handed" means different things to different groups.
- **Pick two hands that share little.** Lines from different sections of the card are a good start.
- **Count to 27.** A quick count after each discard saves both hands.
- **Name your discards out loud.** It's half the fun, and it heads off arguments.
- **Keep the official rulebook nearby** if you play often. It settles the edge cases this guide doesn't cover.

*Pip says: Two racks feel like a lot the first time. By your second game, you'll be planning both hands without thinking about it.*
